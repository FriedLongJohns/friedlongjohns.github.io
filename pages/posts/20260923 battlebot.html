PLANT NHRL Battlebot

I started working on a 1-pound battlebot

Ever watched Battlebots on TV? I think it's pretty fun watching 250-lb robots tear each other apart. So when I heard they've got a similar thing at Purdue...

Awww yeah.

The club I'm in is competes in NHRL. A much lighter game than battlebots, competitions have 3, 12, and 30 lb brackets. For our beginner project, my team is fighting at our school's local Plastic Antweight (PLANT) competition.

Primary design iterations

Our team wanted to build a Melty bot - a type of bot that spins it's entire body as a weapon in order to get much more energy out of it. The more senior members convinced me we should do a ring spinner archetype, so I got to work on a brainstorming version.

Pretty rough, but you might be able to see the frontal brushless motor (weapon motor) driving a belted pulley in order to save vertical space.

1 / 3

Ring spinners are pretty heavy, so we decided to compact the design by fitting the motors much closer together. We considered gears and chains, but both would deal poorly with massive impacts during matches.

I also discovered that the entire top of the brushless motor rotates, so I changed the design to let the motor directly drive the weapon ring with friction, using O-rings mounted on a 3d printed cover.

Do mind the decorations on the direct drive's baseplate.


While our ring was retained with one driving and two bearing wheels along the equator, this leaves the ring vulnerable to up/down attacks like saws or flippers. Most ring bots retain their rings by having vertical bearings as well as horizontal ones, but bearings are heavy and we want to save weight.

So I took some inspiration from a much bigger battlebots brother: The Ringmaster.

First Test Prints

With the ring problem (somewhat) solved, we made some test prints.

You can see the battery access panel splits this top plate design into 2 pieces. Not a good idea, in terms of strength.

The second test print has much of the walls and lower motor blocks directly printed into the bottom plate, and I've even put in the power switch to check it's fit. Most of the screw holes worked first try, fortunately.

1 / 3

You know what didn't work first try?


Diagonal bearings.

We didn't have the right size of axle for the bearings. Or the right sive of bearings for the axles.

With the ring problem (somewhat) solved, we made some test prints.

So Actually, Nevermind About All The Stuff I Said About The Things

I didn't feel like buying more axles when we had perfectly good standoffs, so back to the drawing board the robot went.

1 / 4

The robot got redesigned to use standoff (light grey) mounted to the plate, with a casing (blue) around the bearings (yellow) that allowed them to go into the ring's grooves.

Wheels on the robot are hubs with Polyeurethane cast around them, which is why there's a gigantic mold intersecting the wheel in the CAD view. These are rather heavy, so we ended up sawing one of the wheels in half with a tiny little swiss army knife.

(We only cut ourselves a few times)

Do note the o rings that have fallen off of the weapon motor casing... there's some foreshadowing there.

If you couldn't tell from the wiring spilling out of the robot, we didn't have a ton of space inside the bot. I made a few final changes to the design to eek more space out of the insides, and out prints were off to finish right before competition.

Post-Competition Changes

A few* things happened during our competition.
The screws on top of the weapon casing rubbed against the top plate, so I cut a big chunk out of that.
We went through about 5 o-rings during the competition, due to them slipping off, snapping, and generally being miserable whiny gremlins. To fix that, we swapped the o-rings for another polyeurethane wheel in their place. In addition, the ring shape itself has been strengthened because our rings kept snapping during competition.

A whole lot of tolerances were added and adjusted as well, but nobody credits the .3mm of space on a lego stud for the success of the whole set.
1 / 2

Also, I made a secret version of the robot with gears instead of friction.

Yes, the bearings fit in a tiny groove between the gear layers.

I promise gear teeth are very impact-resistant and would never snap under load.

1 / 3

End?

We may have lost every match, but this was the biggest CAD project I've ever had to do. It taught me a lot about tolerances and screw fitting.

I'll probably be competing next semester as well, perhaps with a full-combat robot. 3 pounds, metal, and a lot more expensive if I forget my tolerances again.
